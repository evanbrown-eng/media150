Reading 3

“Graphic Novel: Motherhood in crisis” by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, Saidu Bah, and Al Jazeera combines text, illustration, photography, and video to tell the stories of hardship and survival for four women in the West African nation of Sierra Leone. In a nation where healthcare systems are fragile, access to basic treatment is limited, and pregnancy often means risking death, these distinct media work together to tell deeply traumatic tales. In one of the stories, “My punishment”, the narrative follows Tetteh, a convicted woman navigating pregnancy-related tragedies both in and out of a state prison. In this particular story, illustration is used to simplify actions, exaggerate certain expressions and capture certain emotional states in which other media can’t capture.
One distinct advantage that illustration provides over text or photography is protection, specifically, protection of anonymity and dignity. There are ethical, safety, and legal risks when it comes to photographing an incarcerated woman discussing the loss and struggles she experienced in a state prison. Illustration in this context acts as a protective shield which obscures Tetteh’s identity while keeping the authenticity and emotional weight of her story. By abstracting her physical likeness, exploitation is avoided and a new opportunity for connection between Tetteh and the reader is opened.
Another distinct advantage comes from the way illustration visualizes trauma, memory, and the unseen. Since a camera can only record physical objects and events in real time, it’s bound to the present and the external. Illustration allows the narrative to step outside of the limits of linear time to depict not only internal reflections and psychological distress, but probably the most important, memories. All the stories in this comic series, not just “My punishment”, are able to step into a non-photographic and even a non-recorded expression of these narratives. Linework, colors and shadows can come together to convey all types of events and experiences, such as rage, powerlessness, and isolation.
While the documentary photos and videos can anchor the reader in realism, carrying the weight of truth, they can also feel narrow, static and almost cold. Photography and video can record the events, the environments, the people, but the illustrations strive to interpret the internal experiences in these moments. By weaving these expressive pieces into this documentary mix, the truth of the narrative becomes multi-layered. It bridges the gap between objective reporting and deep, personal reflection, turning Tetteh’s tales into not just a statistical event, but a human experience.
