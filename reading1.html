Reading 1

Even with the advancements and accessibility of modern photography, we continue to wander in Plato’s cave according to Susan Sontag. Maybe it is only out of habit that we continue to mistake images of truth, for truth itself. Yet, Sontag describes the camera as a tool that both captures and interprets reality. While I first thought that Sontag saw photography as just another shadow along Plato’s cave, further analysis may reveal that it may actually straddle the line between truth and fiction.
The first concept that deepened my understanding was Sontag’s historical description of the camera before the industrial revolution and it’s mass production. She describes a freedom with photography similar to how people used the first home computers. Being “a toy of the clever, the wealthy, and the obsessed”, with no levels of expertise or professionalism, the possibilities seemed endless as it was an art form before being classified as art. Though this raises a question, without the reinforced self-consciousness of the social use of cameras, does this phase of photography bring us closer to truth?
Another concept that furthers this investigation on the dichotomy of photography, is it’s relationship to tourism. As Sontag states, by certifying the experience, by striving so much to capture the undeniable truth of a fun, successful trip, truth is denied. This is because it turns the experience of the tourism into capturing tourism. It becomes less about being mindful and being in the moment, and more about finding the perfect photographic souvenir. But it then begs the question, what if the goal of the trip is to create an album of photos. Sontag also highlights that taking photos can be soothing, giving shape and guidance to travel.
The camera can not only soothe, but it can also grant power and control with it’s almost unquestioned rights to invade, interfere or ignore. The event is subjected to the whims of the photographer and though Sontag first argues that photography is an act of non-intervention, I can’t help but disagree. As she states when talking about Hitchcock’s “Rear Window”, even though there is no physical intervention, the camera still participates in the event. But how does a camera participate? Yes, it can save a moment, but what does it say about it, that warrants a photograph? Does it encourage, admonish or something else entirely? If taking a picture is like what Sontag says, to have interest in it, does it comply with the unchanged state of the subject? I do not always think so. Does a war photographer take pictures to further fuel bloodshed? I cannot say with an absolute answer.
As cameras grow more powerful and it’s reach expands ever outward, a central dilemma remains: will photography ever capture truth in it’s purest form or is our concave lens just an extension of Plato’s cave? Purpose definitely plays a key element in this conundrum. Does art capture truth? Can it? And as photography becomes more widely used for amusement, socialization, and tools for warding off anxiety and gaining power, does truth even a thought for those that take these pictures?
